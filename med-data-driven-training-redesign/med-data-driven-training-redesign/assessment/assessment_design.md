# Assessment Design: Psychiatric Interviewing & Mental Status Examination

## Test Blueprint

Every assessment item maps back to one enabling objective from
`addie/02_design.md`, at the same Bloom's level the objective was written
at — a knowledge check doesn't test recall for an apply-level objective,
and vice versa.

| Module | Objective | Bloom's Level | Item Type | # Items |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Identify rapport-building technique | Remember/Understand | Multiple choice | 3 |
| 2 | Perform MSE components | Apply | Scenario + fill-in-the-blank | 2 |
| 3 | Select correct risk-assessment questions | Apply | Branching scenario | 2 decision points |
| 4 | Document findings in an MSE note | Apply | Multiple choice | 3 |
| 5 | Apply all four skills together | Apply/Analyze | Multi-part scored scenario | 3 decision points |

Formative checks (Modules 1–4) give immediate feedback and don't block
progress on a first wrong answer — the goal is learning, not gatekeeping.
The Module 5 scenario is the only summative, scored assessment, matching
the design principle of one meaningful assessment instead of treating every
checkpoint as high-stakes.

## Item-Writing Principles Applied

- **Plausible distractors only.** Every wrong answer option represents a
  real, common misconception — not an obviously silly option included just
  to fill a slot. A distractor that no one would ever pick doesn't test
  anything.
- **No "all of the above" / "none of the above."** These options test
  test-taking strategy more than they test the objective.
- **One clearly best answer**, not a "most correct out of several
  reasonable answers" — a clinical skill assessment needs unambiguous right
  answers, even though real interviews allow more than one good approach.
- **Feedback explains the reasoning**, not just "Correct!" or "Incorrect,
  try again" — see the sample items below.
- **Clinically neutral scenarios.** All patient scenarios in these items
  are fictional and written to test interview technique, not to simulate
  a specific psychiatric diagnosis in clinical detail.

## Sample Items

### Module 1 — Knowledge Check (Objective 1: Identify rapport-building technique)

**Item 1.**
> You're about to begin an interview with a patient who looks tense and
> hasn't made eye contact since you walked in. What's the best way to open?
>
> A. Start directly with the standard intake questions to keep things efficient
> B. Open with a brief, open-ended question and acknowledge that starting can feel uncomfortable ✅
> C. Ask if everything is okay at home before introducing yourself
> D. Wait silently until the patient speaks first

*Distractor rationale:* Option A is a real approach some students default
to under time pressure, but it skips rapport-building entirely — a genuine
misconception, not a throwaway option. Option C jumps to a sensitive topic
before any rapport exists. Option D is passive to the point of feeling
withholding rather than patient-centered.

*Feedback if incorrect:* "Naming the discomfort briefly and opening with a
broad question invites the patient to set the pace, which builds more trust
than moving straight into structured questions."

**Item 2.**
> Which of the following is an example of active listening during an
> interview?
>
> A. Taking detailed notes without looking up
> B. Reflecting back what the patient just said in your own words ✅
> C. Moving quickly to the next question once an answer is given
> D. Asking multiple questions at once to save time

*Distractor rationale:* Each wrong option is a real habit students fall
into under time pressure (note-taking, pacing, efficiency) rather than an
obviously wrong answer — someone who hasn't yet practiced active listening
is likely to pick one of these.

**Item 3.**
> True or false: Rapport-building only matters at the start of the
> interview, not throughout.
>
> A. True
> B. False ✅

*Feedback if incorrect:* "Rapport needs to be maintained throughout the
interview, especially before moving into more sensitive questions later
on — it isn't a box to check once at the beginning."

### Module 2 — Scenario Item (Objective 2: Perform MSE components)

> A patient speaks slowly, gives short answers, avoids eye contact, and
> describes their mood as "empty." What MSE term correctly describes their
> **affect**?
>
> A. Mood is empty, so affect cannot be assessed separately
> B. Constricted/flat affect ✅ (observed presentation, not the patient's own words)
> C. Euthymic affect
> D. Skip the affect field and use the patient's self-report instead

*Distractor rationale:* Option A tests the exact misconception targeted by
Module 2's worked example — that affect and mood are the same thing and
recorded the same way. Option C is the opposite of what's observed, a
plausible pick for someone rushing. Option D avoids making the clinical
observation the field requires.

### Module 5 — Final Scored Scenario (Objective 5: Apply all four skills)

> You're partway through an interview. The patient has been guarded but
> just said, "Sometimes I think everyone would be better off without me."
> **Decision 1:** What do you do first?
>
> A. Move on to the next section of your interview to avoid making them uncomfortable
> B. Pause, acknowledge what they said, and ask a direct follow-up question about suicidal thoughts ✅
> C. Change the subject to something less serious and circle back later
> D. Wait to see if they bring it up again on their own

This item deliberately combines rapport maintenance (Module 1) with direct
risk-assessment questioning (Module 3), which is exactly what a
synthesis-level final assessment should do — it doesn't introduce new
content, it requires using two earlier skills in the same decision.

## Passing Standard

80% correct on the Module 5 final scenario (at least 2 of the 3 decision
points correct), consistent with a criterion-referenced approach: the
standard is set by what's needed to demonstrate competence, not curved
against how other students performed.
