# ADDIE Phase 2: Design

## Design Approach: Backward Design + Chunking

Starting from what students need to be able to do (not from the content
that currently exists), then building the shortest, clearest path to each
outcome. Each outcome becomes its own short module, following the
chunking principle: shorter, focused units reduce cognitive load and are
easier to fit around clinical rotations than one continuous block.

## Terminal Learning Objective

By the end of this module, students will be able to **conduct and document
a structured psychiatric interview and Mental Status Examination
appropriate to a standardized patient scenario**, as measured by a final
scored scenario assessment.

## Enabling Objectives (ABCD Format)

Each objective follows Audience–Behavior–Condition–Degree, with a Bloom's
taxonomy level noted so the assessment design can be matched to the right
cognitive level (recognition-level content shouldn't get an application-level
test item, and vice versa).

| # | Objective (ABCD) | Bloom's Level |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Given a list of interview opening scenarios, the student will **identify** the appropriate rapport-building technique for each with 100% accuracy. | Remember / Understand |
| 2 | Given a standardized patient scenario, the student will **perform** the core components of a Mental Status Examination (appearance, behavior, speech, mood/affect, thought process/content, cognition, insight/judgment). | Apply |
| 3 | Given a patient presentation, the student will **select** the correct follow-up questions to assess suicide and safety risk, matching best-practice interview protocol. | Apply |
| 4 | Given a completed interview, the student will **document** findings in a structured MSE note, including all required fields. | Apply |
| 5 | Given a realistic, multi-part standardized patient scenario, the student will **apply** rapport-building, MSE components, risk-assessment questioning, and documentation together to conduct a complete psychiatric interview. | Apply / Analyze |

Objective 5 is intentionally the synthesis objective — it's not new content,
it's the four earlier skills used together, which is also why it becomes
the final scored assessment rather than a new module of instruction.

## Module Map

Five modules replace the single 6-hour workbook, each built around exactly
one enabling objective:

| Module | Objective(s) | Length | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Building Rapport & Structuring the Interview | 1 | 12 min | 3-question knowledge check |
| 2. Conducting the Mental Status Examination | 2 | 15 min | Scenario-based quiz |
| 3. Assessing Risk: Suicide & Safety Questioning | 3 | 15 min | Branching scenario |
| 4. Documenting Your Findings | 4 | 10 min | 3-question knowledge check |
| 5. Pulling It Together | 5 | 8 min | Final scored scenario |

Total active learning time: **~60 minutes**, down from 6 continuous hours —
delivered as five short sessions a student can realistically complete
between rotations and coursework, each with its own quick check instead of
one high-stakes final exam.

## Assessment Blueprint (Summary)

Every objective gets checked at the level it was taught, and the full
detail — including sample items and distractor rationale — is in
`assessment/assessment_design.md`. In brief: objectives 1 and 4
(remember/apply-level) get short knowledge checks; objectives 2 and 3
(apply-level) get scenario-based items where the student has to use the
skill with a simulated patient, not just recall a fact; objective 5
(synthesis) gets one scored scenario that draws on all four earlier skills
together.

## Instructional Strategies

- **Worked examples before practice:** each module opens with a concrete
  example (a modeled patient interaction) before asking the student to
  apply anything themselves.
- **Immediate feedback:** every knowledge check gives feedback right away,
  not just a pass/fail — see the interactive prototype in `prototype/` for
  what this looks like in practice.
- **Spaced retrieval:** delivering the five modules over roughly two weeks
  (rather than back-to-back) gives a spacing effect between exposure and
  the final synthesis scenario, which supports retention better than
  massed practice — important for a skill students need to recall under
  pressure with a real patient.
- **Low-stakes, frequent checks over one high-stakes exam:** replacing a
  single end-of-module test with five short checks lowers anxiety around
  any one assessment and gives students (and the curriculum team)
  visibility into exactly where understanding breaks down, module by
  module.
